## How the market ruined visual pleasure

The other day I was talking with a friend about one of the latest updates his phone had received and how it had ruined the aesthetics of his screen. If your phone is heavily customised and you like what you've got, when something messes it up it tends to be pretty annoying. The problem gets worse when the changes they've introduced aren't even reversible, and you're stuck adapting to a new reality (even if it's a digital one) you weren't prepared for.

[![STEM Tweet](../static/images/STEM_twit.png)](https://x.com/stem_feed/status/1673307258846056451)

After a few messages back and forth we arrived at the conclusion that the problem was that, in the industry these days, the people making the decisions have several issues when it comes to aesthetics. For one, they couldn't care less about aesthetics as such; what they want is to ship the product as fast as possible and keep feeding their departments with resources, lest someone takes them away. For another, they want nothing to do with features or pleasant experiences that make us waste our time, since the outcome is what matters. And for a third, they want nothing to do with aesthetics, and they're proud of it. Decision-making is always driven by maximum profit, once again, and the shortest possible production time at the lowest cost, intimately tied together.

{marginnote}I still think that lowest production cost or speed don't have to go hand in hand with cheapness. Not so in the market: whenever you're trying to reach the masses or ship fast, it's intrinsically in the DNA of the solution.{/marginnote}

There was a time when things weren't entirely functional, but they had their aesthetics and they did it well. There are some programs that have kept it, being just as functional as, or more than, commercial products, and giving a place to all those advanced users who want to keep their features and experience. We can talk about the [gnu](https://www.gnu.org/software/software.html) tools, the [suckless](https://www.suckless.org) tools. That they have that name is no coincidence, and in fact this conversation is part of their manifesto ["... quality software with a focus on simplicity, clarity and frugality."](https://suckless.org/philosophy/). But let's not fool ourselves. Simple, clear and sober doesn't mean not useful. The suckless people have managed to twist software to unexpected degrees, where one gets to develop their individuality and personality and lay it down in the form of a desktop aesthetic.

The [enshittification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enshittification) is a pretty dangerous phenomenon. You can be comfortably using your favourite platform, or your favourite app, when suddenly, one day, it passes into other hands and, before you realise, it starts going into entropy. The decay of platforms described in **enshittification** is something that happens because the system can't keep things as they are, statically, and keep generating constant profits at the same time. In a sense, services have to get worse in order to improve, or for new players to show up. It's the same thing that happens with the consumer goods market and planned obsolescence. If you wanted clothes that were quality and durable, you were born in the wrong period. What's economically sustainable is wearing plastic, suffering from skin diseases, and having your clothes last you two news cycles.

Some companies and tools try to step away from this phenomenon, albeit with some caution. Companies like Apple, which have [a certain obsession with the euler curve](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clotoide) and still hold very high quality standards when it comes to design and aesthetics, both in their physical and digital products, find certain nooks to sustain that their phones deteriorate over time through forced updates, and even when it wouldn't be computationally necessary to replace them, they "force" you in some way into swapping them for newer ones. We can also find tools like [novlr](https://www.novlr.org/), for writers. In this case both the website and the program *per se* are pretty pleasing in terms of aesthetics and functionality. Companies or tools like these are few, since it's not always sustainable to maintain them nor to invest if you don't make enough money to stand out, and even less so if you're opensource.

And there are also tools that want to keep being useful but with a minimalist aesthetic. There's [calcurse](https://calcurse.org/) which makes a terminal calendar, simple, with a handful of features, and that does well what it has to do. That is. Not all is lost. Aesthetics still comes first, whether as a way of showing your personality and individuality, and by learning and seeing what you want in other places and sharing and setting your tastes against what the violent market offers you, you can end up with very pleasing things.

{marginnote}The judgment of taste does not found pleasure in any concept, nor in any determinate end, but in the free harmony of the imagination and the understanding, which we feel as freedom in the play of our cognitive faculties. (*Critique of Judgment*, §9){/marginnote}


In conclusion. That the best decisions aren't always taken by the best people, the most prepared ones, in this age of mediocrity. That the market always tries to reach everyone and, to do so, has to be in constant change, even if that change isn't always about improving the experience or getting a better aesthetic. But that if you dig, if you go deep, there are still people who can hold standards that surpass anything industrialised. Digital craftsmanship has its space and always will, even though it keeps getting harder to find in such an industrialised world. Beauty is synonymous with what human beings are and their freedom.
