## We don't know what answer we want for our questions

When you've spent years in forums or circles where doubts get sorted out, whether about software or anything with a minimum of technical involvement in any field, you catch on the fly all those badly formulated questions nobody wants to answer. A priori, this type of question stirs up unease in the forum and, whether because of the mental work they take to tackle or the knowing upfront that everything they're asking you is wrong, they tend to attract the most direct and nasty replies people keep stashed away in their dark drawers.

# The (blessed) XY question problem

To know what the problem is, first we'll have to know what a question is. It seems we've all got that clear, but let's start from the beginning just in case...

From the RAE:

### **Question**: f. An interrogation that is put to someone so they answer what they know about a matter or another thing.

When we ask a question, we want the answer of another person who knows about the subject. We want to acquire the knowledge this person has. The problem with the question itself, anthropologically speaking, is that we assume the one formulating it knows what they want to know. That is, a person asks "What's the temperature like?" because they want to know whether it's cold or hot.

So far, we can all agree. But what happens when a person doesn't know what they want to know? That's where the XY problem is. To stick with the previous example, if that person asking about the temperature really wants to know whether the clothes they're wearing are suitable for the occasion, they're formulating the wrong question. Maybe the occasion is a funeral and knowing the temperature is so they can put on the winter or summer clown suit they wear in their day job. The right question would be whether the winter or summer clown suit is suitable for the funeral, not the temperature.

Recently, on one of the Arch Linux forums (please, keep using the forums and start using Linux) I spent several days with a person who had a question about how to generate different virtual desktops on their computer. The task is simple, since virtual desktops work like windows in the compositor this person was using (the same one I use day to day, Qtile). Once these desktops were created, the person wanted to run several applications on each of the desktops and leave them open, so they could get to them whenever they wanted.

![Qtile desktop](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/archcraft-os/misc-files/main/screenshots/qtile/main.png)

The answer was simple: "You generate them with a list of desktops; if you want 1000 you make a list of 1000". But the person wasn't happy and kept coming up with different explanations about why they wanted them, that they were going to destroy and recreate them dynamically (whatever that's supposed to mean), that they were going to have a dashboard on a website (for some reason they needed that...). And after a lot of conversation, it came out that this person had no idea what they were doing. They didn't want 1000 desktops or 1000 screens with 10 applications open on each screen, but rather what they needed was a server management system for their supposed theoretical application that they were already imagining was massive and didn't even have started yet...

## The basics are fundamental

One of the things I like most about non-commercial websites is how direct they are and how little they care about attracting an audience; they get straight to the point and exit your life as if they were a small pamphlet or a single sheet someone handed you on the street. This concept of "self-defining websites" I like a lot, and it's one of the principles of internet botany. These websites don't want to keep you hooked so you come in every day; they simply have a concept, a task or a duty, they fulfil it and they're happy (in case a website can be happy). An example like this is the [xyproblem](https://xyproblem.info/) website itself. With a .info domain we already know what we've got in the very url. A marvel. Besides, they explain the problem much better than I can, in everyday language and with pretty direct examples. The website has a very straight visual path and has 0 distractions. It's like a small (life) manual where they tell us in a small pill what it is, what it implies and a few examples.

## Communication and the failure of language

It's not trivial to say that we all make mistakes asking XY questions. The psychological meaning is pretty deep and we could get to analyse the problem underlying this style of questioning. Sometimes you can even spot a certain passive aggression in the people who formulate this kind of question, and their predisposition not to accept the answers given. Like a kind of preventive tactic so they don't get answered what they don't want... or something like that. What I think has worked best for me, at least in the corporate world, is going back and asking directly about what they want to build, and from there ramping up the complexity. I don't know if it's a solution for everyone, but at least we'd avoid a lot of misunderstandings.

I don't know if someday we'll get rid of this problem, maybe it's what makes us human. But giving it a name and being able to recognise it at first sight has saved me a lot of stress over the years.
